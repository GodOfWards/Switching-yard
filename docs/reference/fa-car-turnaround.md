# FA freight car turnaround: reference

How long a Ferrocarriles Argentinos freight car took to come back after
1977, how that cycle split between moving and standing, and how freely
broad-gauge cars moved between the Roca, Sarmiento, Mitre and San Martín.
Researched 2026-11-23 for items 3 and 5 of #98. The 1977 figures it
builds on are in [fa-freight-traffic.md](fa-freight-traffic.md#cars).

**Status:** the yearly turnaround by gauge to 1986, FA's average freight
train speed and the free times to load and unload are **Confirmed** on
primary sources (World Bank tables quoting FA; the Reglamento General de
Ferrocarriles). **Nothing per line or per car type was found**; the split
of a cycle and the dwell per yard pass are **Derived** below. How freely
broad-gauge cars ran between lines is **partly answered**: cars belonged
to a line, each line had its own general manager, and empties went back
"en devolución"; no rule or fleet count per line was found.

## Turnaround after 1977

"Turn-around of freight cars (days)", FA's own figures as the World Bank
printed them. Targets are the 1979 Plan of Action's.

| Year | Broad gauge | Target | Standard | Narrow | FA, all gauges (WB 1989 Table 7) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1977 | 19.8 (WB 1979) | | 13.5 | 17.9 | 18.7 (WB 1979) or 20.0 (WB 1983) |
| 1978 | | | | | 22.0 (WB 1983) |
| 1979 | **24.1** | 18 | 17.0 | 24.7 | 25.6 (WB 1983: 23.6) |
| 1980 | **34.7** | 17 | 30.9 | 25.5 | 27.1 |
| 1981 | **27.0** | 16.5 | 19.0 | 17.5 | 27.0 |
| 1982 | **14.5** | 16.0 | 15.0 | 12.7 | 14.5 |
| 1983 | **12.6** | | 20.3 | 13.4 | 12.9 |
| 1984 | **21.3** | | 22.3 | 14.8 | 15.9 |
| 1985 | **23.8** | | 23.2 | 19.5 | |
| 1986 | **21.6** | | 18.6 | 15.5 | |

Source: WB 1989 Table 6 item 12 (by gauge) and Table 7; WB 1983 Table 5;
WB 1979 Tables 2.12-2.13. Confirmed.

- **A broad-gauge car took 21 to 24 days in a normal year of the 1980s,**
  worse than 1977's 19.8 and never near the 16-18 day targets. The 1980
  peak (34.7) is a recession year: 16.3 Mt against 22.5 Mt in 1983.
- **1982-83 are the outliers, 13-15 days,** the years FA carried the most
  (22.5 Mt and 13.4 billion t-km in 1983, against 15-17 Mt in other years,
  WB 1989 Table 7). More traffic meant cars waited less to be loaded.
  This reading is mine.
- **The sources disagree by up to 2 days for the same year** (1977: 18.7
  or 20.0; 1979: 23.6 or 25.6, and Table 7's 25.6 can't be an average of
  Table 6's three gauges, all lower). Use the by-gauge row.
- **Nothing per line or per car type exists in these reports.** FA
  reported by gauge only.
- The 1991 audit sums it up: turnaround "increased from 16.3 days in 1970
  to 20 days in 1976 and 23.8 days in 1985" (WB 1991 ¶3.07).

## Free time to load and unload

From the RGF as it stood in FA's time (articles as amended by Decreto
126/1972 and the 1975 resolution). All Confirmed.

| Fact | Source |
|---|---|
| Stations take freight on working days **06:00-18:00** and Saturdays 06:00-13:00 | RGF art. 309 |
| The railway tells the shipper **24 hours ahead** when the cars will be placed | RGF art. 291 |
| **Loading: 24 hours, counted only while the station is open,** from placing (so two working days). After that the shipper pays *estadía* per tonne of the car's capacity; one who hasn't started loses the car to the next in line | RGF art. 303 (Decreto 2.352/1966) |
| Whole trains (categories A and B, from 1975): **12 working hours** to load the train, from one hour after the cars are placed | Resolución SETOP 352/75, instructions |
| **Unloading: a car is "at the consignee's disposal" 24 hours after it is placed for unloading; then 24 hours to unload** ordinary freight (48 h on two Patagonian lines), counted only in station hours; Saturday afternoons, Sundays, holidays and rain don't count | RGF art. 308, 310, 311 (Decreto 126/1972) |
| Bulk liquids in tank cars: **12 free hours** to load or to unload, unless the tariff gives other | RGF art. 314 |
| Ports: 48 hours to unload; ports get 72 hours to return the empties to the junction | RGF art. 313 |
| *Estadía* is charged per tonne of capacity per 24 hours, rising day by day; the rates are in the tariff book (*Libro Nº 2*), not found | RGF art. 315 |

## Legal transit time

The RGF also caps how long the railway may take to deliver a load. All
Confirmed.

| Fact | Source |
|---|---|
| **24 hours for the first 80 km; 6 hours per further 40 km up to 640 km; 6 hours per further 60 km beyond.** Plus 24 h per junction with another company's track, 24 h for a break of gauge, Sundays and holidays, and **48 hours for delivery** (84 h to or from second-category lines) | RGF art. 253 |
| **All those times +25%** for ordinary tariffs, from 1941 "until art. 253 is amended"; reduced tariffs get their own time +25% of art. 253's | Decreto 83.348/1941, made indefinite by Decreto 25.540/1948 |
| For livestock, the clock starts two hours after loading ends | RGF art. 368 |

So the loaded run alone, with the +25%, may legally take (Derived):

| Distance | Legal transit (h) | Days | At 25 km/h running |
|---|---|---|---|
| up to 80 km | 30 | 1.3 | ≤ 3 h |
| 255 km (Junín-Retiro) | 67.5 | 2.8 | 10 h |
| 517 km (1977 broad-gauge average haul) | 112.5 | 4.7 | 21 h |
| 800 km (Junín-Mendoza, approx.) | 157.5 | 6.6 | 32 h |

Delivery's 48 h (+25%) comes on top.

## Splitting a cycle (Derived)

#98's model: **cycle = fixed time + distance ÷ effective speed**, fitted to
broad gauge 1977: 19.8 days for about 1,030 car-km (two average hauls of
517 km; [fa-freight-traffic.md](fa-freight-traffic.md#cars)).

**What a car spent moving.** At FA's 24-26 km/h, 1,030 km is about 41
hours: **1.7 days of a 19.8-day cycle, about 9%.** The other 18 days the
car stood. Check: 52 car-km a day ÷ 25 km/h = 2.1 hours moving a day.
Derived.

**What the rules allow at the ends.** Placing notice, loading in station
hours, the 24 h before the consignee has it and 24 h more to unload come to
about **2 days at origin and 2-3 at destination** once nights and a
weekend fall inside: roughly **4-5 days a cycle**. Derived.

**What that leaves: about 13 days** of yard dwell, waiting for a train,
empties waiting for an order, and slack. Two fits bound how much of it
grows with distance:

| Fit | Fixed part | Per km (one way) | Rests on |
|---|---|---|---|
| A: only running grows with distance | 18.1 days | 2d ÷ 25 km/h | WB freight train speed. Says a 30 km cycle takes 18 days: too flat |
| **B: transit grows as the RGF's legal time** | **10.4 days** | legal transit ×2 (≈ 0.375 h per km of haul) | RGF art. 253 +25%. Assumes FA ran loads at about its legal time, and empties back as fast |

Fit B, recommended (retunable), gives:

| Haul one way | Cycle, fit B | Cycle, fit A |
|---|---|---|
| 30-80 km | 12.9 d | 18.2 d |
| 100 km | 13.6 d | 18.4 d |
| 255 km (Retiro) | 16.1 d | 18.9 d |
| 517 km | 19.8 d | 19.8 d |
| 800 km (Mendoza) | 23.6 d | 20.8 d |

For the 1980s, scale by year: a 22-day broad-gauge average (1984-86)
gives fixed ≈ 12.6 days with the same per-km part, or keep 1977 as the
game's year. Derived.

**Dwell per yard pass (Derived, rests on an assumption).** Fit B's loaded
leg of 517 km takes 112 h legally, of which about 21 h is running: **about
90 hours standing en route.** If a car passes **three yards each way**
(origin pick-up into a zone yard, one of FA's ~24 concentration yards, the
destination yard; *Assumed*, from WB 1979 ¶2.51's zone plan and nothing
measured), that is **about 30 hours per yard pass**, including the wait
for a train out. No FA figure for yard dwell or yards passed was found.

## How freely broad-gauge cars moved between lines

| Fact | Source | Status |
|---|---|---|
| **FA was run as six railways**, each with a General Manager responsible for day-to-day operations; "inter-railway coordination" sat at headquarters | WB 1979 ¶2.10 | Confirmed |
| An early-1970s plan to cut the six administrations to four (**two broad-gauge subsystems**) was "largely halted" by the unions and "the individual lines" | WB 1979 ¶2.11 | Confirmed |
| Line managers (*Gerentes de Línea*) could change the wagon-supply rules on their line | Res. SETOP 352/75 art. 1 a | Confirmed |
| **Empties moved "en devolución"** (being sent back), and could be loaded on the way "en buen rumbo" (going the right way), out of turn, as long as they weren't diverted out of their set zone | Res. SETOP 352/75 art. 3 and instructions | Confirmed |
| Cars in programmed circuits (*vagones encerrados en circuitos especialmente programados*) were not to be used for other orders | Res. SETOP 352/75 art. 1 b | Confirmed |
| A car with a shifted load is reported with its number "and the railway it belongs to" | RITO art. 415 d (1993 text) | Confirmed, also in [fa-freight-traffic.md](fa-freight-traffic.md#cars) |
| The 1976 unified marking dropped line initials for the FA monogram | [fa-freight-cars.md](fa-freight-cars.md) | Confirmed |
| FA's statistics and the World Bank's targets count the broad-gauge fleet as one (availability, load, turnaround "B.G.") | WB 1979 Table 2.13, WB 1989 Table 6 | Confirmed as reporting, not as operation |
| RGF art. 253-254 add 24 h per junction with "another company" and split transit time between companies; written for the private lines, and whether FA applied it between its own lines wasn't found | RGF | Unconfirmed for FA |
| Fleet counts per line, and loadings per line: **not found** | | |

**Reading (mine, not found stated):** a broad-gauge car could be loaded
anywhere on the broad gauge and run through to another line, but it still
belonged to a line, and once empty it was sent back towards it, picking up
a load only if one was going its way. So a Roca or Mitre car at Junín was
normal, and it would leave empty eastward unless something was loading in
its direction. Partly confirmed; a dated photo of a San Martín train with
other lines' marks, or an FA *Manual de Formación*, would settle it.

## What it means for the game

Not decided here; Tom's calls go in `../decisions.md`.

- The game's away times (8, 12 and 16 days from Junín, per
  [fa-freight-traffic.md](fa-freight-traffic.md#what-it-means-for-the-game)) are shorter than
  every FA figure. Fit B gives about 13 days for a short trip, 16 for
  Retiro and 24 for Mendoza in 1977, 2 to 3 more in the mid-1980s.
- The per-car free time (two working days to load, a day plus one more to
  unload) is a natural in-game deadline for a car spotted at an industry.
- Running time is under a tenth of the cycle, so a game clock that skips
  days spends almost all of a car's absence on "standing somewhere".
